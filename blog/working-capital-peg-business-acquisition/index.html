Working-Capital Pegs: Closing Adjustment and True-Up Examples

A working-capital peg is a target used in a transaction's agreed price-adjustment mechanism. The useful question is not just “current assets minus current liabilities,” but which accounts, accounting policies, dates, and adjustments the actual documents require.

This original website exercise uses fictional figures, not a client closing statement, a valuation, a verified book excerpt, or a contract template. The inclusion rules and price formula below are assumptions for the exercise. They do not determine the treatment of an actual transaction.

Define the accounts before calculating the peg

For this exercise only, included assets are trade receivables net of a specified allowance, inventory net of a specified reserve, and operating prepayments. Included liabilities are trade payables and operating accruals. Cash, debt, and transaction expenses are excluded from this working-capital calculation and handled separately in the assumed price bridge.

Do not copy those exclusions into an actual deal without reviewing its definitions. In the November 13, 2024 Spire/Kpler share purchase agreement filed with the SEC, the defined current assets and liabilities refer to specified line items and transaction accounting principles; the price formula also treats cash, debt, expenses, and working capital separately. This illustrates transaction-specific drafting, not a rule that its terms govern another purchase.

Check how aged receivables affect the peg

A working capital peg is the target level of deal-defined working capital used to calculate a purchase-price adjustment. Apply the same agreed definitions to the historical target and closing balances.

Suppose a closing schedule reports $240,000 of working capital against a $200,000 target. The schedule includes a $60,000 receivable that is 120 days old. If the parties’ agreed definition excludes that receivable and the target already uses the same rule, eligible working capital falls to $180,000. The preliminary adjustment moves from $40,000 above target to $20,000 below target. Check the agreement for how each difference is applied.

Reconcile the aging report to the ledger, record any subsequent collection, and ask how disputed invoices, allowances, and excluded balances are treated. Review historical months under the same definition before treating the peg as comparable.

A $200,000 target and a $150,000 closing balance

The fictional target is $200,000. Assume the agreed method requires the allowance and inventory reserve shown below. The gross balances and reserves must be reconciled separately so that the same reserve is not deducted twice.

Included assets are $160,000 + $75,000 + $10,000 = $245,000. Included liabilities are $70,000 + $25,000 = $95,000. Closing working capital is $245,000 − $95,000 = $150,000.

Under the assumed dollar-for-dollar formula, adjustment = closing working capital − target = $150,000 − $200,000 = −$50,000. The negative sign reduces the assumed price. This is arithmetic under the exercise's terms, not a finding that a real seller owes money.

If the two assumed reserves were omitted, the balance would be $180,000 + $90,000 + $10,000 − $95,000 = $185,000 and the adjustment would be only −$15,000. The $35,000 difference is the combined allowance and reserve. Whether either reserve is appropriate in a real closing depends on the agreed policies and evidence.

Keep working capital separate from the other price adjustments

Assume a $2,000,000 base price, $40,000 separately included cash, $120,000 separately deducted debt, and $8,000 separately deducted transaction expenses. These amounts have not been included in the $150,000 working-capital balance.

Illustrative final price: $2,000,000 + $40,000 − $120,000 − $8,000 − $50,000 = $1,862,000.

Check each account's assigned category. If the same $8,000 expense were already subtracted in working capital and then deducted again in the expense bridge, the price would be reduced twice. This exercise excludes it from working capital. An actual agreement may define categories differently; reconcile its anti-duplication provisions and schedules.

Do not subtract the full final adjustment twice

Suppose estimated closing working capital was $170,000. Its adjustment against the $200,000 target was −$30,000. Holding the other bridge amounts constant, the estimated price was $1,882,000.

Final working capital is now $150,000, with a −$50,000 final adjustment and a $1,862,000 final price. The change from estimate to final is $1,862,000 − $1,882,000 = −$20,000. Equivalently, −$50,000 − (−$30,000) = −$20,000.

Subtracting another $50,000 from the estimated price would produce $1,832,000 and count the original $30,000 reduction again. Do not confuse the full final adjustment with the incremental true-up. Actual settlement timing, amounts paid, escrow, dispute procedures, interest, and other changing components require the actual closing documents.

Averaging periods and seasonal comparisons are choices to review

There is no universal 13-week target established by this guide. For an arithmetic illustration, three comparable historical observations of $180,000, $200,000, and $220,000 average $200,000. A different comparison set of $160,000, $170,000, and $180,000 averages $170,000. At the same $150,000 closing balance, the assumed adjustment changes from −$50,000 to −$20,000.

Those invented sets do not establish which period is appropriate, prove seasonality, or justify choosing the lower target. Review the operating cycle, timing of collections and payments, inventory patterns, unusual items, and the same account definitions across the proposed history and closing balance. Weekly observations and month-end snapshots are different datasets; do not silently substitute one for the other.

What the working-capital review file should contain

  • Controlling definitions: document version, included and excluded accounts, measurement time, and any agreed illustration.
  • Historical bridge: source balances for each observation, consistent classifications, reserves, adjustments, and the proposed target calculation.
  • Closing support: receivable aging and allowance analysis, inventory records and reserve support, prepayment schedules, payable cutoffs, accruals, and relevant reconciliations.
  • Category mapping: show how cash, debt, expenses, taxes, deferred revenue, and other disputed items are treated under the actual agreement. Do not infer a universal treatment from their names.
  • Estimate-to-final bridge: separate the original estimate, final calculation, amounts already reflected in the price, and incremental differences.
  • Review process: identify the preparer, reviewer, access to supporting records, required notices, deadlines, unresolved items, and applicable dispute procedure.

If the documents mention a collar, threshold, basket, cap, or earnout linkage, have counsel and the accounting reviewer establish exactly how it operates. A label alone does not say whether the full difference or only an excess is adjusted. Nor does a later working-capital shortfall automatically authorize an earnout reduction or payment withholding.

Keep operating liquidity planning separate from the purchase-price mechanism. A contractual target is not proof that the buyer has enough cash to operate, fund payroll, or service debt after closing. Review those needs in a separate cash forecast.
