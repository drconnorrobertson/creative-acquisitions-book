Acquisition Funding Plan: Cash, Conditions, and Repayment

An acquisition funding plan should show who supplies each amount, what the amount can fund, when it becomes available, and what obligations continue afterward. Adding more instruments does not by itself make an acquisition affordable or eligible for a loan. Start with the actual uses, then reconcile the proposed sources and their conditions.

This is an original website review guide, updated November 22, 2026. The numbers below are fictional assumptions, not a completed transaction, financing offer, market-rate benchmark, or verified book excerpt.

  • Reconcile the funding bridge
  • Check conditions before counting cash
  • Separate payments from closing funds
  • Check SBA restrictions
  • Keep a reproducible review

A fictional $1.5 million project

Assume $1,300,000 of purchase consideration, $50,000 of transaction cash costs, and $150,000 of initial working cash. Uses total $1,500,000. The proposed sources are $900,000 senior borrowing, $250,000 buyer cash, $200,000 partner equity, and a $150,000 seller note. They also total $1,500,000.

That equality does not make all $1,500,000 spendable cash. Assume the seller note is noncash purchase consideration. Cash sources are $900,000 + $250,000 + $200,000 = $1,350,000. Cash uses are $1,150,000 seller cash consideration + $50,000 costs + $150,000 working cash = $1,350,000. The note accounts for the remaining $150,000 of consideration on both sides.

This example does not assume an SBA loan or establish that any source is approved, cleared, unrestricted, or suitable. No rates, ownership percentages, repayment terms, tax treatment, or lien rights are supplied by the arithmetic.

Check each source before treating it as available

  • Senior borrowing: identify the borrower, approved amount, permitted uses, expiry, funding conditions, payment schedule, guarantees, and collateral documents. A discussion or term sheet is not proof of disbursement.
  • Buyer cash: document the amount and source, actual availability date, permitted use, and liquidity left after closing. Borrowed funds may create another repayment obligation and need separate eligibility review.
  • Partner equity: distinguish a proposed contribution from completed documentation and verified funds. Review the security, capitalization, governance, distributions, and any offering requirements separately. A dollar contribution alone does not establish an ownership percentage.
  • Seller note: identify the deferred consideration, payment restrictions, interest, maturity, security, and priority. Do not count deferred consideration again as cash available to pay costs.
  • Rollover consideration, if proposed: reconcile seller equity proceeds and new units separately. Twenty percent of a seller's equity proceeds is not automatically twenty percent of enterprise value or of the new company. See the rollover proceeds and ownership example.

The funding availability exercise shows a balanced proposal with a remaining cash gap. Keep unresolved funding conditions visible rather than filling the gap with an unverified contribution.

A funding bridge does not test future payments

The $150,000 seller note reduces assumed closing cash consideration, but it may create later principal and interest payments. Record every obligation on a dated payment schedule. Identify balloons, rate changes, permitted distributions, reserve needs, and periods when payments overlap. Test operating cash after supported expenses and other cash needs against that schedule.

Likewise, deferred or contingent consideration is not automatically cheaper. Compare cash timing, total modeled payments, downside liquidity, ownership dilution, and contractual risk under explicit assumptions. Do not infer a lower economic price or automatic tax savings merely because less cash changes hands at closing.

Do not carry a general funding illustration into an SBA approval

The current SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026, contains specific ownership-change rules in Appendix 15. Seller debt proposed for equity treatment must meet full-term standby and subordination requirements; a two-year payment holiday is not the full-term requirement. The baseline limited-source cap and any applicable notice also need review.

Appendix 15 prohibits seller earnouts in covered changes of ownership. It also restricts an equity investment by a standby-debt provider. Do not assume that a seller note, retained seller equity, and a contingent payment can be combined just because they each appear in a general acquisition discussion.

Have the lender classify the transaction and review the actual source mix, documentation, valuation, and repayment capacity. The SBA project-cost guide and standby document guide address those questions separately. This fictional bridge is not a lender-approved structure.

Keep a reproducible version of the plan

  1. Define the acquisition perimeter, price, cash costs, working cash, and other uses from supporting records.
  2. List each proposed source once; distinguish cash from noncash consideration and proposed from verified amounts.
  3. Record conditions, timing, responsible reviewers, and any remaining funding gap.
  4. Review repayment, ownership, guarantees, priority, legal and tax treatment, and financing restrictions as separate questions.
  5. When terms change, update sources, uses, and payment assumptions together. Preserve the prior version and explain the change.

A complete arithmetic bridge is one review result. It is not proof of eligibility, acceptable risk, investment suitability, enforceability, or readiness to close.
