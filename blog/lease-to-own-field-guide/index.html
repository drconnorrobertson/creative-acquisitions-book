Lease-to-Own Acquisitions: Payments and Option Credits

A lower monthly payment can leave a larger exercise balance. Reconcile what you pay, what conditionally earns purchase credit, and what remains due before comparing proposals.

Reviewed November 22, 2026Creative Acquisitions website guide

This is an original fictional website exercise, not a verified excerpt from the book, a client transaction, or a financing offer. The amounts and conditions below are assumed proposal terms, not legal rules. A proposal does not establish an executed agreement, ownership, or approved funding.

Keep paid cash, earned credits, and exercise cash separate

For proposal A, assume a $360,000 option exercise price, an $18,000 upfront option fee, and twelve monthly payments of $6,000. Only $2,000 per month earns price credit, usable on valid exercise under the assumed terms. The upfront fee has no price credit in this proposal.

  1. Cash paid before exercise: $18,000 + (12 × $6,000) = $90,000.
  2. Conditional price credits: 12 × $2,000 = $24,000.
  3. Remaining exercise cash: $360,000 − $24,000 = $336,000.
  4. Total nominal cash through exercise: $90,000 + $336,000 = $426,000.

The full $72,000 of monthly payments is not a $72,000 purchase credit. The $18,000 fee is also not silently subtracted a second time. These totals exclude operating expenses, financing costs, transaction fees, and taxes.

Compare timing as well as totals. B reduces cash before exercise by $12,000 but increases exercise cash by $12,000 compared with A. The equal $426,000 nominal totals do not establish equivalent present value, affordable financing, or equal operating risk. C is a different proposal: the $18,000 fee credit must be separately documented; it is not a default feature of lease-to-own arrangements.

Test a missing credit and a no-exercise branch

D assumes every monthly payment was paid, but only eleven $2,000 credits met the assumed eligibility condition. Credits fall from $24,000 to $22,000 and exercise cash rises by $2,000. This is a sensitivity example, not a conclusion about any actual default, forfeiture, cure right, or enforceability.

If A is not exercised after all twelve payments, $90,000 was paid under these facts. That amount is not automatically an economic loss: the value of use, possible refunds, continuing obligations, and exit terms have not been determined. Do not label it acquired equity or assume it must be returned. Read the actual agreement with the appropriate advisers.

Build a review record before choosing a proposal

  • Define what can be purchased. Identify the assets, entity interests, equipment, premises rights, intellectual property, and exclusions actually described. Do not assume a business option transfers a property lease or another contract.
  • Identify the controlling documents. Record each document's name, version, parties, signatures, and effective date. Separate a term sheet or draft from an executed obligation.
  • Trace every payment and credit. Match scheduled amounts to receipts, credit eligibility conditions, any caps, and a running credit ledger. Identify disputed or unearned amounts rather than counting them as available.
  • Confirm fee treatment. Is the upfront amount refundable, credited on exercise, separate consideration, or subject to another term? Record the clause and adviser review; do not infer the answer from the label “option fee.”
  • Reconcile the exercise balance. Document the applicable exercise price, adjustments, credit total used once, exercise deadline and notice requirements, and estimated separate closing costs.
  • Assign operating responsibilities. Ask who pays payroll, maintenance, insurance, taxes, working capital, and unexpected repairs during the term. Include those cash needs in a separate operating model.
  • Record unresolved approvals. Identify required landlord, lender, seller, regulatory, or other consents where relevant. A modeled balance is not evidence that any consent or financing has been secured.

The SBA's buying-an-existing-business guidance suggests considering an attorney and accountant and reviewing records such as contracts and leases, financial statements, tax returns, and the sales agreement. That general diligence guidance does not supply this exercise's payment terms or determine legal, tax, or loan eligibility outcomes.

State the decision the records can support

A useful comparison says: “Under proposal A's assumed terms, the twelve-payment schedule requires $90,000 before exercise and $336,000 at exercise, before excluded costs.” It does not say the buyer owns the business, has a tax deduction, qualifies for an SBA loan, or can fund the exercise balance.

For an actual proposal, have counsel review the rights, conditions, consents, and exit mechanics and have an accountant review the cash and tax treatment. Resolve missing terms before treating a spreadsheet balance as an executable transaction.

Questions about the exercise

Does every lease payment reduce the purchase price?

Not in this exercise. Proposal A pays $6,000 monthly but earns only $2,000 of conditional price credit per month. The actual agreement must establish which payments qualify and when credits can be used.

Is the option fee automatically credited at exercise?

No such assumption is made here. Proposal A leaves the $18,000 fee outside price credits; proposal C separately assumes an agreed $18,000 fee credit. Confirm the actual documented terms rather than importing either example.

Does the lowest monthly payment mean the lowest total cost?

No. Proposals A and B each total $426,000 of nominal cash under the stated assumptions, but allocate cash differently before and at exercise. This comparison does not measure present value, financing costs, tax, or operating results.
