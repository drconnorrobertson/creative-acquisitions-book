The Letter of Intent: How to Write an LOI That Gets Deals Done

A letter of intent (LOI) records proposed acquisition terms and the process for deciding whether to proceed. Its title does not establish which provisions are enforceable, whether exclusivity exists or whether either party must close. Review the full document, incorporated agreements, signatures and governing law with transaction counsel before relying on it.

Do not classify obligations from the heading alone

The parties should expressly address which provisions they intend to bind, which remain proposals and what requires a later definitive agreement. Ask counsel to reconcile that language with confidentiality, exclusivity, expenses, access, termination, dispute provisions and any agreement to negotiate. Do not assume only confidentiality and exclusivity can matter.

In SIGA Technologies v. PharmAthene, Delaware Supreme Court, May 24, 2013, the court addressed an express contractual obligation to negotiate in good faith in accordance with a term sheet. It held that such an obligation was enforceable under Delaware law; on the required factual findings, expectation damages could be available. The case involved particular agreements and facts. It does not make every LOI binding or supply a universal rule for another state's law.

This example is a reason to review the interaction of documents, not a substitute clause. An obligation during negotiation, an agreement on proposed economics and an obligation to complete an acquisition are different questions. Preserve the document versions and communications needed for counsel's review.

Describe the proposed transaction precisely

Identify the proposed buyer and seller, asset or equity structure, included and excluded interests, price and adjustment mechanisms. Separate cash at closing, seller debt, contingent amounts and any assumed obligations. A headline purchase price does not explain the cash each party must provide or receives.

If seller financing is proposed, list the amount, payment structure, priority, security and outstanding approval questions. For a contingent price, define the proposed metric, measurement period and information rights for counsel's review. If SBA financing is contemplated, do not assume a seller earnout is available: the current change-of-ownership rules prohibit it. Review the current SBA SOP and SBA acquisition guide with the lender.

Working-capital terms need a defined scope, measurement date, accounting approach and treatment of debt-like items. Do not assume cash belongs in every peg or that a trailing average is automatically appropriate. Use the working-capital review to list the questions rather than treating a one-line target as a complete adjustment mechanism.

Tie access and deadlines to actual dependencies

Discuss the records, people and premises needed for diligence, who may receive information and how confidentiality will be handled. A request list does not itself authorize contact with employees or customers. Coordinate the financial review scope, legal and tax work, financing and operational review before promising a timetable.

If exclusivity is proposed, identify its scope, start, end, prohibited activities, exceptions, notice requirements and any extension or termination mechanism. Neither a signature nor an assumed industry timeframe proves that exclusivity exists. A proposed period should fit the agreed work and the seller's position; there is no universal minimum number of days for every acquisition.

List proposed closing conditions and responsibility for financing, diligence, consents, approvals and definitive documents. A condition is not necessarily satisfied because someone expects it to be. Review consequences of delay, withdrawal, failed conditions and termination, including provisions that may survive. Distinguish a target date from a binding deadline.

A signed LOI is one record in a longer process

Retain the signed version and referenced agreements, confirm the operative dates with counsel, and track amendments, notices and unresolved conditions. Do not assume draft language survived into the executed document. Before a deadline or proposed extension, check the actual notice and approval process.

Seller-transition support should follow the business's needs and negotiated terms, not an automatic consulting period. Separate proposed availability from a signed scope, compensation and tested handoff. The transition review and operating cash exercise provide related records.

A signed LOI does not establish committed financing, successful diligence, effective ownership transfer or a guaranteed closing. Use it to expose unresolved questions while the parties can evaluate them.
