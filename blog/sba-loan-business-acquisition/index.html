SBA Acquisition Financing: Project Costs and Repayment Review

Buying a business with an SBA-backed loan requires a review of the acquisition, the borrower, the funding sources, and repayment capacity. A small cash contribution or a balanced spreadsheet does not establish eligibility. This guide separates those decisions and uses an original fictional example to reconcile project costs, cash, and seller debt.

Policy review: November 22, 2026. Start with the SBA's 7(a) overview and the current SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026, especially Appendix 15 on changes of ownership. Have the lender confirm the transaction classification and any later notices before relying on a funding structure.

What the program does and what the lender must review

The 7(a) program guarantees qualifying loans made by lenders; it does not give every buyer an approved acquisition budget. SBA lists complete and partial changes of ownership among permitted uses and a maximum loan amount of $5 million. Applications go through a participating lender. Eligibility includes creditworthiness and the ability to repay; the lender reviews the actual business and transaction.

Ask the lender to identify whether the proposal is an Initial Acquisition, Business Expansion, Owner Buyout, or another covered transaction. Do not apply one down-payment percentage, earnings multiple, credit-score cutoff, or debt-service test to every acquisition. A seller's advertised earnings figure is a starting document to investigate, not verified cash available for debt payments.

Total project uses are $1,050,000 + $50,000 + $100,000 = $1,200,000. Under the baseline Initial Acquisition requirement in Appendix 15, 10% of this assumed project cost is $120,000. Calculating 10% of the purchase price alone would give $105,000 and miss $15,000 of the assumed injection requirement. The lender must confirm which actual costs belong in the project and the applicable classification.

A seller note is not cash deposited at closing

In the baseline rules, limited equity sources together can supply no more than half of the required injection. Here, the proposed $60,000 standby note is half of $120,000; the assumed $60,000 buyer cash supplies the other half. That demonstrates the arithmetic only. It does not prove that the note, buyer funds, loan amount, valuation, or transaction qualifies.

Cash sources are $1,080,000 + $60,000 = $1,140,000. Cash uses are $990,000 paid to the seller + $50,000 costs + $100,000 working cash = $1,140,000. The $60,000 seller note is a noncash source and noncash consideration. Counting it again as spendable cash would overstate liquidity.

Seller debt used for equity treatment must be subordinated and on full standby: no principal or interest payments during the 7(a) loan's term. Accruing interest is a separate matter from paying it. Review the standby note terms guide before presenting a note as part of the injection.

A separate Veteran Business Acquisition notice, effective November 11, 2026, permits a different source mix for qualifying transactions. It does not waive the minimum injection. Qualification, timing, ownership, control, and documentation must be checked against the notice; veteran status alone is not an approval. The fictional example above uses the baseline mix, not that exception.

Stress the payment budget separately

For a separate annual cash exercise, assume $150,000 of senior debt payments. This is an input, not a rate quote or a payment calculated from the proposed loan above. Assume $210,000 of cash available before debt in the base case and $165,000 in the downside. Neither figure is verified earnings or the lender's calculated SBA debt-service numerator.

Any paying note needs its own lender-approved role and terms. At $165,000 of cash and $170,000 of payments, the model is short $5,000 before additional excluded cash needs.

These cash-budget ratios are not an SBA underwriting calculation or approval test. Appendix 15 sets different debt-service coverage requirements by transaction type and specifies the financial evidence and adjustments. Have the lender reconcile tax returns, financial statements, debt schedules, proposed payments, and supported adjustments. Owner compensation, taxes, capital spending, seasonal needs, and other obligations still need explicit treatment in the actual budget.

Prepare questions the lender can resolve

  • Classification and timing: which ownership-change category applies, which current policy and notices govern, and what remains subject to review?
  • Uses and valuation: reconcile price, costs, working cash, and other uses to documents; identify the valuation and financial diligence required for this transaction.
  • Sources: identify the source of buyer cash, every investor or debt contribution, standby terms, and the records needed to verify each amount.
  • Repayment: list every debt payment and its start date, proposed rate and term, permitted seller payments, supported earnings adjustments, and downside assumptions.
  • Legal obligations: review guaranties, collateral, lien priority, consents, purchase documents, and continuing seller obligations with the responsible advisers. An SBA guarantee to the lender does not cancel the borrower's debt.
  • Open conditions: record the reviewer, missing evidence, next action, and updated budget whenever financing or acquisition terms change.

Do not add a seller earnout as an assumed SBA financing shortcut. Appendix 15 prohibits seller earnouts in covered changes of ownership. It treats buyer rebates and working-capital adjustments separately. Ask the lender and transaction counsel to classify the actual provision before including it in a proposal.

Questions about the worked example

Does a 10% injection guarantee an SBA acquisition loan?

No. The example reconciles a baseline Initial Acquisition injection assumption. Eligibility, valuation, repayment, permitted uses, source verification, current policy, and lender approval remain separate reviews.

Can the full-standby seller note make interest payments?

Not under the full-term standby assumption used for equity treatment here. Accrual does not authorize cash payments during the 7(a) term.

Why can a balanced funding plan still have a repayment shortfall?

Sources and uses address funding at the modeled closing. The annual repayment cases test a different period. Case C has $165,000 before debt and $170,000 of payments, leaving a $5,000 shortfall.
