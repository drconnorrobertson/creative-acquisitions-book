Seller Financing: Payments, Security, and Tax Review

Seller financing means part of the consideration remains payable to the seller after closing under agreed terms. Review the amount deferred, payment schedule, interest, security, priority, and remedies before treating that deferral as a solution to a funding gap. Neither a secured note nor a seller guarantee is automatic merely because the parties use the phrase “seller financing.”

This website guide was reviewed November 22, 2026. It provides questions for an actual transaction review, not a lender approval, standard rate sheet, tax-savings estimate, or promise that a seller will accept deferred payment.

  • Separate closing cash and future debt
  • Read the payment and security terms
  • Check the tax assumptions
  • Review other financing
  • Separate transition obligations

A lower closing payment leaves an obligation to review

A seller note can defer a portion of the consideration. It does not eliminate that obligation or prove that the business can fund it. Reconcile cash paid at closing, noncash consideration, future principal, interest, and any contingent payments separately. Record transaction costs and working cash instead of assuming the purchase price is the entire funding need.

Then build a dated payment calendar. The 36-payment seller-note exercise illustrates a fictional loan whose monthly payments leave a substantial maturity balance. Its assumed rate and term are exercise inputs, not recommended or typical seller-financing terms.

Read the documents behind each payment

  • Principal and timing: what amount is deferred, when is each payment due, and does maturity leave a balloon?
  • Interest: what rate, accrual convention, compounding, and rate-change provisions apply? Distinguish accrual from permission to pay.
  • Security and guarantees: who owes the debt, what collateral or guarantee is actually documented, and which interests or claims have priority?
  • Restrictions: do standby, subordination, lender consent, or other provisions restrict payments, amendments, enforcement, or prepayment?
  • Default and disputes: what notices, cure periods, review procedures, and remedies do the actual documents provide? Do not assume a right to seize the business or offset a disputed amount.
  • Cash capacity: how do payments interact with senior debt, owner compensation, taxes, capital spending, seasonal needs, and reserves?

A negotiated interest rate, note term, or cash contribution should be stated as a proposal with its assumptions and conditions. This guide does not supply universal ranges or establish a market rate.

Deferral of payment is not an automatic tax reduction

IRS Publication 537, Installment Sales, explains the installment method and its exceptions. A sale of a business can involve multiple assets with different treatment. Inventory is not eligible for installment reporting, and depreciation recapture generally must be reported in the year of sale even when cash is received later. Interest also needs separate treatment.

Have the tax advisers review the seller, assets or interests sold, basis, allocation, recapture, interest, payment timing, and applicable elections and limitations. Do not promise lower total tax, full gain deferral, or enough closing cash to pay the tax solely because a seller accepts a note.

Other financing can change the note's role

If an SBA loan is proposed, distinguish a note counted toward the injection from other seller debt. The current SOP 50 10 8.1, Appendix 15, requires full-term standby and subordination for seller debt considered equity. A two-year payment holiday is not that full-term requirement. Source limits and any applicable notice must also be checked.

Review the standby note document guide with the lender before offering payment terms. Seller financing does not automatically remove bank underwriting, consent requirements, legal review, or funding uncertainty elsewhere in the transaction.

A note does not substitute for a transition agreement

A delayed payout does not prove that the seller will provide introductions, training, cooperation, or continuing operating support. State transition deliverables, dates, access, acceptance evidence, and compensation in the actual agreed documents. Keep them separate from assumptions about the seller's incentives.

Use the transition handoff review to identify open responsibilities, and the payment calendar worksheet to record the proposed schedule. Neither worksheet authorizes a payment or determines legal rights.
