Customer Concentration in an Acquisition: Read the Revenue Schedule Carefully

Customer concentration is a question about dependence: how much of a business's recorded activity comes from particular customers, and what would change if that activity changed? A percentage is a useful starting point, but it cannot establish whether a relationship will continue, whether a contract transfers, or whether a proposed purchase price is reasonable. Those conclusions require different evidence. Start by making the calculation understandable before using it in an acquisition discussion.

This guide uses an entirely fictional customer schedule to show the calculation, the limits of the calculation and the questions worth taking to the people reviewing an actual transaction. It replaces the idea of a universal acceptable percentage with a more useful discipline: define the records, reconcile the total, explain customer grouping and investigate material dependencies. Nothing in the example describes a real customer, lender decision, acquisition result or reviewed valuation.

The practical output is a short customer-dependence explanation that someone else can reproduce. It should show the period, denominator, account grouping and unresolved evidence. It should also distinguish historical revenue concentration from receivable collection, customer profitability and future retention. Combining those subjects under one reassuring score can conceal the very risk the buyer is trying to understand.

Define the period and the measure first

Write a sentence such as: “This schedule summarizes the supplied customer revenue export for the twelve months ending September 30.” Then identify whether the export contains recognized revenue, invoices, orders or cash receipts. These measures can differ. A deposit collected this month, an invoice issued last month and revenue recognized under the business's accounting policies are not interchangeable merely because each is associated with the same customer.

Use one defined measure for the initial schedule. If you need a second measure, create a second labeled view and reconcile the difference. Do not silently combine paid invoices with unpaid orders to make a fuller-looking number. The IRS recordkeeping resource linked below explains the role of transaction summaries and supporting records; it does not validate this fictional schedule or resolve the accounting treatment of an actual sale.

Identify the currency and treatment of credits, refunds, sales taxes and intercompany amounts. The teaching file uses positive USD revenue amounts under a deliberately simple convention. An actual export may require adjustments, negative transactions or multiple currencies. Obtain an explanation from the responsible accounting team instead of forcing unfamiliar records into the example's design. Preserve the original export and work on an authorized copy.

Choose the customer grouping deliberately

A customer identifier is an accounting-system label, not necessarily an independent source of demand. Several account numbers might belong to the same economic group. Conversely, a billing intermediary might represent purchases from many independent end users. Decide which question you are asking before grouping the rows. Keep the original customer account identifier alongside any proposed parent or relationship identifier.

For example, two fictional divisions could order separately while relying on one corporate purchasing decision. Presenting them as independent customers might understate dependence on that decision. But grouping unrelated franchisees solely because their names share a brand could overstate it. Verify the actual relationship rather than assuming a shared word in the name establishes common ownership, contractual control or coordinated buying behavior.

Show both views when the distinction matters: account-level revenue and the supported grouped view. Explain the grouping rule and who supplied the relationship information. A reviewer should be able to return from a grouped total to the underlying rows. Unverified parent relationships belong in an unresolved question, not in a definitive ownership chart. Keep sensitive identity information in the protected diligence environment.

Work through the fictional revenue schedule

The downloadable worksheet has five fictional customers. Customer A records USD 400,000, B records USD 250,000, C records USD 150,000, D records USD 120,000 and E records USD 80,000 for the same illustrative period. Their sum is USD 1,000,000. These are teaching amounts, not statements about a business for sale. There are no subsidiaries or account-group adjustments in this simplified file.

Customer A's share is 400,000 divided by 1,000,000, or 40 percent. The top two customers together represent 650,000 divided by 1,000,000, or 65 percent. The top three represent 800,000 divided by 1,000,000, or 80 percent. Those calculations identify which relationships deserve explanation in this particular dataset. They do not establish a universal approval threshold, a financing outcome or an appropriate purchase-price multiple.

Keep the numerator and denominator visible when presenting the percentages. “Forty percent” is ambiguous without the period and measure. It could describe revenue, invoices, receivables, units or gross profit. Write “Customer A: USD 400,000 of USD 1,000,000 supplied period revenue, 40 percent.” A clear label is more valuable than a sophisticated-looking chart that makes the underlying definition difficult to find.

Reconcile the denominator before interpreting the ranking

Compare the customer schedule total with the relevant control total from the source system for the same measure and period. A mismatch is a question to investigate, not a reason to scale every customer row proportionately until the numbers agree. Potential explanations include excluded accounts, report filters, credit treatment, date boundaries or differences between invoice and accounting reports. None should be selected without evidence.

Record the reconciliation bridge. If the customer export totals USD 980,000 while the comparable control total is USD 1,000,000, the unexplained difference is USD 20,000. Do not call the schedule complete while that difference remains unresolved. Identify the missing or differently classified records, document the explanation and rerun the calculation. The fictional download already reconciles by construction; that is not evidence that a real export will.

An apparently small difference can still matter to the ranking. Missing activity might belong to the largest account, a new customer or a group of small customers. Keep unresolved amounts out of confident statements about diversification. If the source lacks enough detail, label the schedule provisional and explain the restriction on its use. Completeness is an evidence question that arithmetic alone cannot settle.

Compare periods without changing the definition

Use comparable periods and consistent grouping rules when looking for a trend. A full year and a short seasonal quarter answer different questions. If a customer accounts for a larger share during a busy project quarter, that observation does not automatically describe the full year's dependence. Identify the business's seasonality and the coverage of the available records before drawing conclusions from apparent changes.

Suppose fictional Customer A remains at USD 400,000 while total revenue rises to USD 1,250,000 in a second comparable scenario. Its share becomes 32 percent. The percentage declined because the denominator grew, not because A became a smaller absolute relationship. Alternatively, if A falls to USD 300,000 and total revenue falls to USD 750,000, its share remains 40 percent despite the lost revenue. Show amounts and percentages together.

Investigate changes in account coding, acquisitions, discontinued products and large one-time projects. A rename can look like customer loss followed by customer acquisition when it is one continuing relationship. A new grouping policy can create a trend that is entirely presentational. Document the mapping between periods rather than treating every system label as an independent customer. Preserve uncertainty when historical mappings cannot be established.

Separate revenue dependence from profitability

The largest revenue customer is not necessarily the largest contributor to profit. Serving a customer can involve distinct product costs, labor, delivery, support, discounts and contractual obligations. A revenue concentration schedule does not contain enough information to calculate those effects. Use a separate, supported customer-margin analysis when appropriate, with a clear explanation of cost allocation and any limits in the underlying records.

Avoid estimating customer profit by applying the business's overall margin to each customer's revenue without labeling it as an assumption. That shortcut can conceal different product mixes or service requirements. If a reviewer uses an illustrative margin scenario, show it separately from actual accounting information. A scenario can help formulate questions; it should not be presented as a discovered fact about an account.

Also distinguish gross margin from operating profit and cash available to a buyer. General overhead may not change when an account is lost. Some direct expenses might decline only after a delay or require contractual commitments to end. These timing and cost questions belong in a separate operational and financial review. The revenue shares in this guide are not a complete earnings model.

Review receivables as a separate exposure

Revenue concentration and receivable concentration can point to different dependencies. A customer may represent a large portion of annual sales but pay promptly, while another has a smaller revenue share and a large overdue balance. Obtain the appropriate receivables view for the actual review date and distinguish issued invoices, disputes, credits and collections after that date. Do not infer collectability from the revenue ranking.

A receivables schedule also needs its own denominator and grouping policy. If receivables are USD 200,000 and Customer A owes USD 90,000, its receivable share is 45 percent under that fictional scenario. That is separate from its 40 percent revenue share in the teaching example. Neither percentage is a probability of default. Confirm the accounting context with the responsible professional reviewing the records.

The linked SEC-filed disclosure illustrates that a public company's own report can discuss major customers and receivable-related concentration separately. It is a company-specific primary disclosure, not an SEC endorsement or a private-acquisition standard. This guide does not import that company's thresholds, risk-management practices or industry economics into the fictional business. Use the source to understand the distinction, not to establish a rule for every transaction.

Investigate what supports the relationship

Ask what evidence supports continued demand from an important customer. Relevant materials may include authorized contracts, order history, service records, renewal correspondence and explanations from responsible managers. A long relationship is useful context, but its duration does not prove future retention. Similarly, a signed document requires interpretation in its actual contractual and commercial setting. An account summary cannot establish the legal effect of its terms.

Identify who performs the work and who maintains the relationship. The seller might hold a personal relationship, a team might manage service delivery, or the customer might primarily value a product or process. These situations call for different transition questions. Avoid assuming that transferring introductions transfers purchasing decisions. Ask how the business currently knows what the customer values and what problems remain unresolved.

Keep speculative explanations separate from source-supported statements. “The customer buys because of our quality” might be a management hypothesis. A dated service-review record or documented purchasing requirement is different evidence. Both can inform a discussion if labeled honestly. The goal is to understand dependence without turning optimistic descriptions into commitments the customer has never made.

Respect confidentiality and contact authority

Do not contact customers merely because their names appear in a diligence file. The seller, transaction advisers and relevant agreements should establish the permitted process for customer contact and information use. A buyer's legitimate question does not itself authorize outreach, disclosure of a proposed transaction or access to confidential account information. Seek a defined process appropriate to the actual transaction.

When a permitted discussion is arranged, agree on its purpose, participants and topics. Separate operational continuity questions from requests for promises about future purchasing. Record what was actually said, who said it and any limits on relying on it. A friendly conversation is not equivalent to contractual consent, renewal or a commitment to maintain revenue. Do not rewrite it as one in a deal memo.

Use anonymized working identifiers where appropriate and keep the identity mapping protected. Avoid distributing customer names through unrestricted spreadsheets, public AI tools or broad email threads. The downloadable example contains invented identifiers and amounts only. Its purpose is to practice interpretation without exposing a real relationship. Actual confidentiality obligations and disclosure decisions belong with the people authorized to manage the transaction.

Treat structure proposals as questions for the deal team

An earnout, holdback, seller note or transition arrangement can change who bears particular contractual and economic risks. None of those labels automatically preserves a customer, creates a right to withhold payment or makes an acquisition affordable. Their effects depend on the actual terms, measurement definitions, enforceability, financing constraints and facts. Ask qualified transaction counsel and financial advisers to review the specific proposal.

For example, a proposed customer-retention payment raises questions about the measurement period, included accounts, credits, pricing changes, reporting access and dispute process. A revenue-based metric can behave differently from a margin-based metric. This guide does not supply legal clauses or recommend either choice. It helps a buyer identify what must be understood before treating an arrangement as an answer to concentration.

Likewise, do not assume a lender will accept or reject an acquisition because one percentage crosses a number found online. Provide accurate customer information to the actual financing provider and ask about the applicable review requirements. Obtain current, transaction-specific answers. This page deliberately avoids claims about universal SBA thresholds, guaranteed lender flexibility or standard changes to equity requirements.

Make a limited, useful decision note

A concise note can begin with the verified measure and period, then state the customer grouping policy, reconciliation status and principal observations. Include the largest account's amount and share and any relevant comparable-period change. Follow with unresolved questions about evidence, operations, contracts and collections. Finish by naming the next authorized review step rather than issuing an unsupported overall risk grade.

For the fictional example, a suitable note says that Customer A represents 40 percent of the supplied USD 1,000,000 period revenue. It adds that the file contains no margin, contract or collection evidence, so it cannot support a retention forecast or valuation conclusion. That is a more useful statement than “high risk, reduce price,” because it tells the team exactly what is known and what remains to investigate.

Keep revised versions traceable. If customer mapping or source totals change, identify the revision and its effect on the percentages. Do not overwrite the old explanation without noting why the conclusion changed. A reproducible calculation and a clear evidence boundary make collaboration easier even when reviewers disagree about the ultimate commercial decision. The schedule should support judgment rather than impersonate it.

Read a partial-loss scenario without overstating it

A buyer may want to understand a reduction in the largest customer's purchases rather than assume the entire account disappears immediately. In a separate fictional scenario, reduce Customer A's revenue by USD 100,000 while leaving every other customer's supplied amount unchanged. Total revenue becomes USD 900,000 and Customer A becomes USD 300,000. Its new share is one third, or approximately 33.33 percent when rounded to two decimal places.

The concentration percentage falls from 40 percent even though the scenario loses USD 100,000 of revenue. That is not diversification through successful new sales. It is a smaller numerator and a smaller business under the selected assumptions. Explain the cause when showing the chart. A declining concentration percentage can accompany an undesirable outcome, so a lower percentage should not automatically be colored green or called an improvement.

This scenario also says nothing about the profit effect, collection of outstanding invoices, speed of replacement sales or costs of changing capacity. Those items require separate assumptions and evidence. Label the row as hypothetical and preserve the original historical view beside it. If someone changes another customer's amount to model replacement sales, identify that as an additional assumption rather than blending it into the same calculation without explanation.

A useful review question is therefore specific: which supported operating and commercial facts would we need to understand this particular revenue reduction? The answer may lead to customer conversations, margin review or operational planning through authorized channels. It should not leap directly from the arithmetic to a purchase-price deduction or a recommendation to proceed.

Frequently asked questions

What customer percentage is automatically too high?

This guide does not establish an automatic acceptable or unacceptable percentage. A revenue share describes the supplied data under a stated definition. Its commercial significance depends on the actual relationship, economics, contracts, alternatives and transaction context. Ask the responsible advisers and financing provider about the particular business instead of treating an online threshold as a rule or financing commitment.

Should related customer accounts be combined?

Combine accounts only under a documented grouping policy supported by evidence about the relationship relevant to your question. Preserve the account-level view and a mapping to the grouped view. Shared names do not prove common purchasing control, while different account numbers do not prove independent demand. Label unresolved relationships rather than making an ownership or control claim from the export alone.

Does a forty percent revenue share mean forty percent of profit is at risk?

No. The fictional 40 percent calculation concerns supplied period revenue only. Profit depends on relevant costs, product mix, pricing and other factors not contained in the worksheet. Cash timing and fixed commitments require separate analysis as well. Do not multiply revenue by a general margin and present the result as an established customer-profit or acquisition-affordability conclusion.

Can a contract eliminate customer concentration risk?

A contract can provide important evidence and rights, but its actual effect requires review of the specific terms and circumstances. It does not turn a historical percentage into a retention guarantee. Have authorized counsel review relevant provisions and distinguish legal rights from operational performance, purchasing behavior and collection risk. This worksheet does not interpret agreements or determine whether rights transfer.

May I contact major customers during diligence?

Use the process authorized for the actual transaction and confidential information. A customer list does not grant permission to contact customers or disclose a proposed sale. Coordinate with the responsible seller and advisers, define the purpose of any permitted contact and preserve the difference between a discussion, a representation and a contractual commitment. Do not use this guide as outreach authorization.

What should I do if the customer schedule does not reconcile?

Keep the difference visible and investigate the underlying reports, filters, dates and adjustments. Do not proportionately alter customer amounts merely to force agreement. Preserve the original records, document supported corrections and identify unresolved coverage. Until the relevant control total and customer schedule are reconciled, describe the results as provisional and explain the limits on decisions made from them.
