Asset vs. Stock Acquisitions: Allocation and Basis Examples

An acquisition comparison should separate what is being transferred, what the documents require, and how the transaction is treated for tax purposes. “Asset deal” and “stock deal” are starting labels, not a complete liability review or a tax calculation.

This original website exercise uses fictional prices, asset values, and basis figures. It is not a client transaction, a verified book excerpt, a valuation opinion, a completed tax filing, or legal advice. No structure is declared universally better for either party.

  • Define the transfer
  • Compare two asset allocations
  • Separate stock and company-asset basis
  • Check election assumptions
  • Review obligations and consents
  • Build the comparison file

Start with the actual property and interests transferred

An asset-purchase review identifies the particular property, rights, and assumed obligations described in the documents. An equity-purchase review identifies the interests in the entity being acquired and the entity's existing records and obligations. Neither description establishes that every desired contract, permit, employee arrangement, or property right can continue without further review.

Keep legal form and federal tax treatment as separate questions. Entity classification, transaction steps, applicable elections, and other facts can change the tax analysis. Do not infer the result from a document title alone.

Two fictional allocations of the same $1,000,000 price

For this exercise, assume a qualifying purchase of a business's assets for $1,000,000, with no transaction-cost, assumed-liability, contingent-price, or other adjustments. The first six classes use assumed values for the identified assets, with the remainder assigned to goodwill. The values are invented to demonstrate a reconciliation; they are not appraisals or elective amounts a buyer may assign at will.

In the base case, classes I–VI total $900,000, leaving $1,000,000 − $900,000 = $100,000 of residual goodwill. In the revised case, an independently assumed $50,000 increase in inventory value raises the earlier-class total to $950,000, leaving $50,000 of goodwill. Both columns reconcile to $1,000,000.

The second column does not create another $50,000 of purchase price or a $50,000 tax saving. It changes the assumed allocation of the same price. Actual classification, fair values, allocation order, basis adjustments, recovery methods, placed-in-service facts, and tax treatment require professional review.

The file contains seven fictional class rows and blank reader-review fields. It is not Form 8594 or a completed valuation.

The IRS instructions for Form 8594 describe the asset classes and residual allocation method. They generally require both parties to report a qualifying business-asset transfer when the buyer's basis is determined wholly by the amount paid; applicability and exceptions must be reviewed. The instructions identify goodwill separately from other qualifying section 197 intangibles.

A share purchase has a different basis question

For a separate simplified stock-purchase illustration, assume a buyer pays $1,000,000 for corporate shares, has no additional basis adjustments, and makes no election or transaction step that treats the purchase as an asset acquisition for tax purposes. Assume the corporation's aggregate existing asset tax basis is $350,000.

Under these stated assumptions, the buyer's modeled cost basis in the acquired shares is $1,000,000, while the company's modeled asset basis remains $350,000. They are different records. The IRS basis guidance in Publication 551 explains cost basis; the figures here assume no additional adjustments. The buyer has not paid only $350,000 for the shares, nor does the share purchase by itself allocate the $1,000,000 among the company's assets in this illustration.

The $650,000 arithmetic difference is not an immediate deduction, a tax bill, or a measure of the better deal. An actual comparison needs the entity's asset-basis schedules, buyer's stock-basis records, seller's basis and gain analysis, transaction costs, applicable elections, and other relevant facts.

Do not turn “no automatic asset step-up” into “no exceptions”

The IRS instructions for Form 8023 describe section 338 elections for qualified corporate stock purchases and the resulting deemed-asset-sale treatment. Eligibility, required parties, timing, and consequences differ by election. The existence of the form does not establish that a particular buyer or target qualifies, that an election has been made, or that its tax cost is acceptable.

This guide does not determine eligibility under section 338 or another election, calculate deemed-sale taxes, or recommend an election. Have the tax team identify the actual structure, necessary consents, filings, basis effects, and consequences for each party before using an asset-basis assumption in a model.

An asset label is not a clean-liability conclusion

Do not conclude that no historical obligation can affect the buyer simply because the documents are called an asset purchase. Have counsel identify the actual assumed and excluded obligations, applicable successor or statutory issues, liens, notices, consents, indemnity coverage, and available remedies. A contractual allocation between parties and rights asserted by a third party are separate review questions.

For an equity purchase, review the entity's records and obligations rather than assuming diligence found everything. For either form, determine whether contracts, leases, licenses, permits, and other rights require consent or additional steps. A price allocation does not prove transferability, release a lien, resolve an employment issue, or authorize a regulated activity.

What a useful asset-versus-stock comparison should contain

  • Transfer map: entities, owners, assets or interests, retained property, assumed obligations, and controlling document versions.
  • Consideration bridge: price, cash, debt, working-capital adjustments, contingent amounts, expenses, and any other relevant components, each counted once.
  • Basis records: buyer cost assumptions, company asset schedules, seller basis records, proposed allocations, and valuation support.
  • Tax-treatment review: entity classification, asset classes, recovery assumptions, gain character, elections, required filings, and separate estimates for each party.
  • Legal and operational review: obligations, liens, consents, employees, contracts, permits, transition work, and unresolved conditions.
  • Decision record: which facts are verified, which remain assumptions, who owns each review, and what must be resolved before a proposal becomes executable.

A lower modeled buyer tax cost does not by itself establish the seller's result or the negotiated price. Compare cash timing, operating needs, financing, legal obligations, and transaction-specific tax effects together. This exercise supplies a reconciliation method, not a ranking of structures or a guaranteed tax outcome.
