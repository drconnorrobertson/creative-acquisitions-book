Seller Financing: Stress-Test the Balloon Payment Before Closing

Map seller-note payments, maturity, refinance assumptions, and downside cash flow before using a balloon structure in a business acquisition.

A seller note can make the initial cash requirement look manageable while leaving a large payment at maturity. The balloon is a financing event that needs its own plan. It should not disappear behind a low monthly-payment figure in the acquisition model.

Write the obligation in plain language

Record original principal, interest rate, payment frequency, payment start date, amortization schedule, maturity date, and the balance due at maturity. Distinguish a note amortized over a longer period from a note actually due over that same period. Ask counsel and the lender to confirm the executed terms and how the note interacts with other financing.

Also identify any variable rate, payment deferral, mandatory prepayment, guarantee, security interest, or standby requirement. These provisions can change cash demands or available remedies. A term sheet is a starting point for review, not the final source of payment obligations.

Calculate the balance instead of guessing

Consider a fictional $300,000 note with 8% annual interest, monthly payments based on ten-year amortization, and a balloon after three years. Assuming standard monthly amortization, the monthly payment is approximately $3,640 and the remaining balance after 36 payments is approximately $233,500.

The payment amount is only part of affordability. Saving enough to cover that balloon evenly across 36 months would require roughly $6,487 per month before considering interest earned, taxes, or other uses of cash. This simple savings comparison is not a required contractual reserve; it exposes how much of the principal remains unpaid.

Test three repayment paths

  • Operating cash: project the cash actually available after payroll, reinvestment, other debt, and owner requirements.
  • Refinancing: identify the lender's likely documentation and underwriting process, while treating future approval, rates, and valuation as uncertain.
  • Asset sale or outside equity: state what must be sold or raised, the expected timing, and what happens if proceeds are delayed.

Do not count the same dollar twice. Cash retained to fund the balloon cannot also fund equipment replacement or a distribution. Show the competing uses in one schedule.

Make the downside case specific

Reduce operating cash, delay the refinance date, and test a higher financing payment. Then ask whether the company can continue operating without relying on a discretionary extension from the seller. If an extension is important, have the transaction team address it in the documents rather than assuming goodwill will solve maturity risk.

Review the note alongside the seller-note payment calendar. The SBA's acquisition-planning resources encourage cash-flow diligence. The example here is arithmetic for evaluating a proposed structure, not a financing offer or a conclusion that a particular lender will accept it.

Reconcile the first 36 payments and the maturity month

This original fictional website exercise expands the example above. It is not a verified book excerpt, client note, lender payoff quote, financing offer, or approval. Assume a $300,000 opening principal, fixed 8 percent annual rate divided by twelve, end-of-month payments based on 120-month amortization, and maturity immediately after the 36th regular payment.

For this ledger, round the calculated regular payment to $3,639.83 and each month's interest to cents using half-up rounding. Principal paid equals the regular payment minus that month's interest. Ending principal equals opening principal minus principal paid. No fees, extra payments, late payments, daily interest, irregular periods, taxes, or other charges are modeled.

Include the regular payment and the remaining balance

Under this exercise's timing, the maturity month contains the regular $3,639.83 payment plus $233,528.56 remaining principal: $237,168.39 of modeled debt cash that month. The balloon is measured after payment 36; do not subtract that regular payment again from the already-reduced balance.

Total modeled note cash through maturity is $131,033.88 of regular payments plus the $233,528.56 balloon = $364,562.44. This equals original principal plus the modeled $64,562.44 interest. A real payoff amount can include additional interest, charges, or other terms; obtain a dated payoff statement rather than treating this CSV as one.

A payment can fit while a reserve plan does not

For a separate fictional monthly budget, assume $40,000 of collections, $25,000 of operating cash outflows, and $5,000 of senior-debt cash payments. Before the seller note, cash remaining is $10,000. After the regular $3,639.83 seller payment, it is $6,360.17. These assumed outflows do not establish a complete operating forecast or debt-coverage ratio.

A purely illustrative $6,487 monthly balloon-saving target would exceed that remaining cash by $126.83 per month. If all 36 deposits could actually be made, $6,487 × 36 = $233,532, which exceeds the modeled balloon by $3.44. The budget above does not demonstrate the ability to fund those deposits. Saving targets are not extra contractual payments unless the actual documents require them.

Without a previously funded reserve or another source, the same $10,000 before seller-note cash in the maturity month would leave $10,000 − $237,168.39 = −$227,168.39. This is an identified funding gap under the assumed budget, not proof of actual default. Do not count a reserve deposit twice as both available operating cash and balloon funding.

Check the terms before using the schedule

  • Reconcile the actual principal, rate, payment frequency, first payment date, amortization period, and maturity date to the controlling documents.
  • Identify the interest convention, rounding rules, payment application, fees, prepayment terms, and any irregular periods. This exercise uses only the conventions stated above.
  • Record which balances are before and after each payment. Tie any extra principal reduction to a verified payment record.
  • Obtain a dated payoff quote and review notice, release, collateral, guarantee, priority, and lender requirements with the responsible advisers.
  • Keep the note schedule, operating forecast, reserve plan, and uncertain refinancing assumptions separate but reconciled.

The CFPB's mortgage amortization explanation distinguishes interest from principal reduction. That supports the general arithmetic distinction used here; it does not make consumer-mortgage rules applicable to a business seller note or establish this fictional note's terms.
