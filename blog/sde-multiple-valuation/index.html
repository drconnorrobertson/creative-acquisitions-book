SDE Valuation Review: Add-Backs, Multiples, and Buyer Cash

A seller's discretionary earnings figure is a proposed adjusted earnings measure. Before multiplying it by a price factor, establish the period, accounting basis, owner role, starting income, and support for every adjustment. A multiple cannot repair an unsupported earnings number or show how much cash a buyer will retain after debt payments.

This original website exercise was reviewed November 22, 2026. All business amounts, adjustment treatments, replacement costs, and multiples below are fictional assumptions. They are not a client transaction, market comparables, an appraisal, a loan approval, or a verified book excerpt.

  • Define the earnings measure
  • Review each adjustment
  • Separate earnings and multiple assumptions
  • Build a separate buyer cash budget
  • Record the evidence and limits

Define what the earnings number includes

In an owner-operator review, a proposed SDE calculation may add back compensation for one working owner and identified discretionary, financing, noncash, or nonrecurring items. The definition and adjustments must be stated. An expense is not automatically removable because it benefits the owner, occurred once, or appears under an accounting label.

Ask whether the starting income already excludes interest, depreciation, taxes, or owner compensation. Adding back the same item twice overstates the measure. Distinguish an adjustment to historical earnings from a future replacement cost, a capital requirement, and a change in working capital.

A buyer who will hire someone to perform the seller's work faces a different operating budget from a buyer who performs that work personally. Adding back the seller's compensation does not make replacement labor free. Likewise, adding back depreciation does not demonstrate that equipment will never require replacement.

A fictional add-back review

Assume the starting $120,000 net income includes every expense listed below. The seller proposes adding them all back. For this exercise, the reviewer instead allows only $3,600 of a $14,400 vehicle expense and leaves a $22,000 legal expense unadjusted because the packet does not establish that it will disappear. These are invented review outcomes, not tax classifications or findings about a real seller.

Hold the multiple still before changing it

At an expressly assumed 3.0× factor, the seller proposal gives $280,400 × 3 = $841,200; the reviewed earnings assumption gives $247,600 × 3 = $742,800. The $98,400 difference comes solely from the $32,800 earnings change multiplied by three. It is not evidence that either amount is a fair purchase price.

These factors are not current industry ranges or selected comparable transactions. A market comparison needs evidence about closed versus asking prices, transaction date, business size and characteristics, earnings definition, included assets, debt, cash, working capital, real estate, and payment terms. Do not apply the same multiple to differently defined SDE, EBITDA, revenue, or cash-flow numbers.

Specify what the resulting amount is meant to price: assets, an operating business, an enterprise value, or an equity interest. A multiplication alone does not establish that perimeter. Debt, cash, retained assets, working-capital adjustments, fees, and deferred consideration can change the amount funded or received. The seller proceeds and ownership guide illustrates a separate enterprise-to-equity bridge.

Build a separate buyer cash budget

For a second, explicitly simplified exercise, start with the reviewed $247,600 figure. Assume $85,000 for replacement labor including its modeled employment costs, $20,000 for annual capital cash needs, $15,000 for an increase in working cash, and $110,000 of acquisition principal and interest payments. These are invented future-budget inputs, not costs established by the historical adjustment review.

The arithmetic is $247,600 − $85,000 = $162,600; less $20,000 and $15,000 leaves $127,600; less $110,000 leaves $17,600 before excluded taxes and other cash needs. This is a simplified bridge, not a complete cash-flow statement, owner take-home estimate, or lender debt-service calculation. Actual timing and any other income-to-cash differences must be reconciled.

If the replacement-labor assumption rises by $20,000, that same bridge ends at −$2,400. A positive adjusted earnings number can therefore coexist with a cash shortfall under the stated costs and financing. Resolve operating roles and cash needs before describing the earnings measure as money freely available to a buyer.

Keep the valuation review tied to records

  • Historical reconciliation: match periods, entities, accounting basis, tax returns, financial statements, and supporting schedules. Explain differences rather than selecting the most favorable total.
  • Adjustment support: record the original expense, amount included in starting income, reason proposed, evidence, recurrence question, and reviewer treatment. Check double counting.
  • Transferability: investigate customer concentration, contract continuity, owner tasks, staffing, asset condition, and ongoing costs. A generic quality label does not supply a price adjustment.
  • Comparable evidence: record the actual transaction terms and earnings definitions before drawing a multiple comparison. Keep asking prices and completed sales distinct.
  • Purpose and scope: identify the valuation date, interest or assets valued, intended use, assumptions, and unresolved evidence. A lender's valuation requirements are a separate review.

The SBA's business-planning guidance, in its buying-an-existing-business section, identifies several valuation approaches and recommends objective investigation with professional assistance. It does not prescribe the invented factors used here. Have the relevant advisers determine the appropriate methods and evidence for the actual transaction.
