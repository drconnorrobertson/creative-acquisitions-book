Seller Equity Rollover: Proceeds, Ownership, and Dilution

A seller's rollover percentage and ownership percentage in a new acquisition entity can be different numbers. Keep enterprise value, seller equity proceeds, the rollover amount, and the new capitalization table separate before comparing proposals.

This original website exercise uses fictional financing and unit terms. It is not a client transaction, a verified book excerpt, a valuation, an investment recommendation, or approved financing. All examples exclude fees, taxes, interest, operating cash changes, other price adjustments, and preferred rights unless expressly stated. An actual rollover's legal, tax, lending, and securities treatment requires review of its specific structure and documents.

  • Reconcile seller equity proceeds
  • Balance sources and uses
  • Calculate the new ownership
  • Test a later unit issue
  • Compare two exit cases
  • Review the actual rights

Start with seller equity proceeds, not enterprise value

Assume the parties use a simplified enterprise value of $2,000,000, existing debt to be repaid of $500,000, and no cash or other price adjustments. In this exercise, the seller equity consideration is $2,000,000 − $500,000 = $1,500,000.

Assume the seller rolls $300,000 of that consideration into the new entity. That is 20 percent of $1,500,000. Cash consideration to the seller is $1,500,000 − $300,000 = $1,200,000. Calculating 20 percent of the $2,000,000 enterprise value would instead produce $400,000 and use a different denominator.

Balance the acquisition sources and uses

Assume $1,000,000 of new acquisition debt, $700,000 of buyer cash equity, and the seller's $300,000 noncash rollover. The old $500,000 debt is paid off. The rollover appears on both sides of the reconciliation as noncash equity consideration; it is not an extra cash receipt.

Total sources and uses each equal $2,000,000. Cash sources and cash uses each equal $1,700,000. The hypothetical new loan is not evidence that a lender has approved the structure or that any guarantee, consent, or eligibility requirement is satisfied.

Use the new equity capitalization to calculate ownership

Assume the new entity issues one class of common units at an agreed $1,000 per unit: 700 units for the buyer's $700,000 contribution and 300 units for the seller's $300,000 rollover. There are no other units, options, warrants, incentives, or preferences in this simplified capitalization.

The seller owns 300 ÷ 1,000 = 30 percent of the new common units. The buyer owns 70 percent. Thus the seller rolled 20 percent of the old equity consideration but owns 30 percent of the new common capitalization. Dividing $300,000 by the old enterprise value would give 15 percent, which is not the new ownership result under these assumptions.

The assumed unit issue price is a proposal term, not an independently verified fair value. In an actual transaction, review the entity, class, price per unit, fully diluted denominator, and economic rights before converting dollars into ownership percentages.

A later unit issue changes the denominator

Assume a later, separately authorized $500,000 buyer contribution buys another 500 common units at the same fictional $1,000 unit price. The seller receives no additional units. Total units rise from 1,000 to 1,500, while seller units stay at 300.

Seller ownership becomes 300 ÷ 1,500 = 20 percent. The buyer now holds 1,200 units, or 80 percent. This is an isolated dilution example; it does not establish a right to issue units, the correct future issue price, whether the seller may participate, or whether any protection applies.

Enterprise value is not a seller distribution

For an independent exit sensitivity, assume $3,000,000 of exit enterprise value, $800,000 of debt to repay, and no other adjustments. The simplified residual pool is $2,200,000.

  • Before the illustrative dilution: a 30 percent pro rata common share of that pool is $660,000.
  • After the illustrative dilution: a 20 percent pro rata common share of the same pool is $440,000.

Holding the pool constant isolates the effect of the percentage. It does not forecast the return on the later $500,000 investment or model what happened to that cash. Neither amount is a promised distribution, an IRR, an after-tax return, or proof that a sale can occur.

For a downside sensitivity, assume $1,000,000 of exit enterprise value and $1,100,000 of debt. The simplified common distribution pool is max($1,000,000 − $1,100,000, $0) = $0. The model therefore assigns no common distribution. That zero does not discharge unpaid debt or decide anyone's liability, guarantees, priority, or legal recovery rights.

Review the rights that the percentage does not explain

  • Security and entity: what exactly is issued, in which entity, for what contribution, and under which signed documents?
  • Economic priority: are there preferred distributions, return-of-capital provisions, participation rights, fees, or other steps before common holders share a residual?
  • Capitalization: does the denominator include incentives, options, warrants, convertible instruments, reserved units, or future commitments?
  • Governance: what voting, board, information, consent, or operational authority is actually granted? An economic percentage does not answer every control question.
  • Future funding: what rules govern unit issuance, participation, dilution, capital calls, and the consequences of not contributing?
  • Transfer and exit: what restrictions, repurchase terms, drag/tag provisions, valuation processes, or liquidity limits apply?
  • Separate obligations: keep transition services, employment, seller notes, earnouts, indemnity, guarantees, and financing conditions distinct from the rollover itself.

The SEC's Investor.gov stock guide explains that common holders may receive nothing after higher-priority claims consume liquidation proceeds. That general risk explanation does not establish the rights of a particular private rollover security. Read its governing documents and actual distribution waterfall.

Do not infer automatic tax deferral, a reduced personal guarantee, loan eligibility, or seller cooperation from the word “rollover.” Have the appropriate advisers review the specific entity, transaction steps, security terms, tax treatment, financing documents, and ongoing obligations. An ownership model helps identify questions; it does not approve the transaction.
