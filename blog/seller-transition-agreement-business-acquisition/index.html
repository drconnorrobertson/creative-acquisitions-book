Seller Transition Agreements: Handoff Terms and Evidence

A seller transition plan should identify the work needed after closing and the evidence that the receiving operator can use it. An introduction, an uploaded procedure, and a demonstrated task answer different questions. Keep those records distinct when deciding what remains open.

This is an original website guide with a fictional six-record exercise, not a verified book excerpt, client outcome, contract template, or legal opinion. The exercise does not establish ownership, payment entitlement, customer retention, or compliance for an actual acquisition.

  • Define the work and authority
  • Review six handoff records
  • Separate completion and acceptance
  • Plan the review sequence

Define the work, authority, and operating boundaries

A transition arrangement may be documented within a purchase agreement, a service schedule, or another agreement. Ask counsel which documents control your transaction and how they interact. Do not assume the label “consulting agreement” decides employment status, tax treatment, decision rights, or enforceability.

  • Scope: name the customer introductions, vendor contacts, procedures, training sessions, and system-access work actually required. Identify exclusions and dependencies.
  • Responsible people: name the person delivering each task, the receiving operator, the reviewer, and the escalation contact. Distinguish access to perform work from authority to approve it.
  • Schedule and availability: document dates, time commitment, response expectations, location, and any reduction in support. Choose these from the actual work and agreed obligations rather than treating a sample duration as standard.
  • Evidence and review: specify what demonstrates delivery, how exceptions are recorded, who reviews the result, and how unresolved items are handled.
  • Compensation and costs: identify proposed fees, reimbursable expenses, invoicing, and payment conditions. Have the appropriate advisers review classification and tax treatment; neither a spreadsheet nor a job label establishes them.
  • Authority and third parties: establish the actual decision rights and required approvals under the controlling documents. An introduction is not proof that a contract, license, lease, or account can be transferred.
  • Changes and exit: ask how the scope can change, how disputes or unavailable people are handled, and which obligations continue when support ends.

Separate delivery, demonstration, and acceptance

T01 supports an introduction having been recorded; it does not establish continued customer business. T03 supports one routine task having been demonstrated within the sample; it does not prove all workflows can operate independently. T02, T04, T05, and T06 each have an explicit unresolved review item.

For these six records, four have an explicit open item and two have a recorded introduction or demonstration. Calling the latter two “33 percent of the business transferred” would be misleading: the tasks are not equally weighted, the packet is incomplete, and no whole-business acceptance standard is supplied. Record what each observation supports rather than turning row counts into business-readiness scores.

A reviewer can record a proposed next action without accepting a contractual deliverable. Keep the agreed acceptance criterion, the observed evidence, the reviewer's decision, and any authorized sign-off in separate fields. If a seller supplies a procedure but the buyer cannot carry out the relevant task, record both facts and the unresolved dependency.

Do not infer a right to withhold a payment

A missing handoff record does not by itself establish that a buyer may withhold a seller-note payment, an earnout, an escrow release, or compensation. Review the actual agreements with counsel, including any conditions, notice and cure provisions, dispute procedures, lender restrictions, and required approvals. Do not invent a cross-default or payment linkage that the documents do not establish.

Likewise, an internal worksheet cannot decide whether a restrictive covenant is valid or what restrictions may be agreed. Use the applicable professional review for that question instead of copying a universal non-compete rule from an acquisition article.

Use a review sequence rather than a default duration

  1. Before closing: identify the proposed transition scope, responsible people, dependencies, and unresolved document terms. Keep draft assumptions visibly separate from agreed obligations.
  2. At the start of the agreed support period: confirm the actual schedule, access process, contact routing, and evidence location. Do not put credentials in a shared handoff worksheet.
  3. During delivery: record introductions, training, sample execution, exceptions, and outstanding approvals. Adjust a plan only through the authorized change process.
  4. Before reducing support: review unresolved tasks and the receiving operator's relevant demonstrations. A calendar date alone does not prove that knowledge transfer occurred.
  5. At the agreed review point: document the authorized review outcome and any remaining obligations. Treat payment, legal release, and operational readiness as separate questions governed by their actual requirements.

No fixed 30-, 120-, or 180-day period is recommended by this exercise. Required work, business needs, actual contractual obligations, and adviser review determine the appropriate plan.

Questions before sign-off

  • Which version of each document controls, and what remains a draft?
  • What exactly was delivered, to whom, and where is the dated evidence?
  • Which tasks were demonstrated independently, and which only have documents or introductions?
  • Which consents, access rights, dependencies, or decisions remain unresolved?
  • Who has authority to accept a deliverable, change the scope, or resolve an exception?
  • Have the relevant advisers reviewed legal, tax, employment, lender, and payment questions rather than inferring answers from this worksheet?

The SBA's buying-an-existing-business guidance suggests considering an attorney and accountant and reviewing records including contracts and leases, financial statements, tax returns, and sales documents. That general diligence guidance does not prescribe this exercise's six records or decide the terms of an actual transition.
