Buying a Business With No Money Down: Check the Cash Gap

“No money down” can describe no buyer cash paid toward the purchase price. It does not establish that transaction costs, working cash, deposits, reserves, or future debt payments disappear. Before evaluating a proposal, write down exactly which cash requirement the phrase excludes and who supplies every remaining amount.

This original website exercise was reviewed November 22, 2026. The amounts and funding states below are fictional assumptions, not completed acquisitions, verified commitments, approved loan structures, market terms, or a promise that a seller will finance a transaction.

  • Define the cash requirement
  • Compare four funding states
  • Review the obligations that remain
  • Check the SBA boundary
  • Keep a complete review record

Zero buyer cash is different from zero project cash

Assume a business purchase price of $600,000, transaction cash costs of $30,000, and initial working cash of $70,000. Total project uses are $700,000. If the seller accepts a note for the entire $600,000 price, the seller consideration is deferred; the other $100,000 still needs a cash source.

The seller note is not cash deposited to pay those costs. With no buyer cash, partner cash, borrowing, or other verified cash source in the exercise, the plan has a $100,000 funding gap. Do not call it fully funded merely because the purchase-price line is covered.

A zero gap does not approve the investment, documents, seller repayment, or operating plan.

Deferral, assumption, and retained equity need different reviews

Seller debt

Review the principal, interest, accrual method, payment dates, maturity balance, restrictions, security, priority, and remedies in the actual documents. Full price deferral does not demonstrate that future business cash can support the note. The seller-note payment exercise shows how regular payments can leave a significant maturity obligation.

Assumed obligations

Taking over proposed lease, equipment, vendor, or other obligations does not by itself establish ownership, release the seller, supply required consents, or show positive equity. Identify the actual transfer, creditors and counterparties, balances, liens, guarantees, arrears, and required agreements. Investigate both recorded and potential obligations with the responsible advisers.

Seller rollover

A retained seller interest does not automatically give the buyer full operating control. Review units, ownership percentages, voting and consent rights, distributions, future funding, and exit provisions. Do not infer collateral security or a guaranteed recovery from an equity interest. The rollover guide separates seller proceeds from new-company ownership.

Outside equity

A partner contribution may bring ownership, governance, distribution, documentation, and securities questions. It is not free capital merely because the buyer contributes no personal cash. The partner funding review distinguishes proposed amounts from cash treated as available under stated conditions.

Do not present this as an approved SBA workaround

The four states above do not assume an SBA loan. For a proposed SBA acquisition, Appendix 15 of SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026, sets transaction-specific requirements. Seller debt considered equity requires subordination and full-term standby, with no principal or interest payments during the 7(a) term. A two-year payment holiday is not that full-term requirement.

The baseline limited-equity-source cap and any applicable notice must also be reviewed. The policy restricts equity investment by a standby-debt provider and prohibits seller earnouts in covered ownership changes. Do not combine a seller note, retained equity, and contingent payments solely because a generic financing illustration lists them.

Have the lender confirm the transaction type, current rules and notices, source verification, project costs, valuation, repayment capacity, and documents. The SBA financing guide and standby document review explain those questions. A small or zero personal cash contribution is not proof of loan eligibility.

Record what “funded” actually means

  1. Define the price and all other uses, their timing, and the evidence supporting each amount.
  2. Show cash and noncash sources separately. Record who supplies each amount and which conditions remain open.
  3. Reconcile the cash gap without counting a note or rollover twice as available cash.
  4. Build a dated future-payment and operating-cash budget; identify replacement labor, capital needs, taxes, reserves, and downside assumptions.
  5. Review legal rights, consents, ownership, financing eligibility, and tax treatment separately with the relevant advisers.
  6. Preserve the proposal version, the reviewer, and the next action when costs or terms change.

Continue with the acquisition funding plan, earnings and buyer cash review, and seller transition handoff guide. A financing label cannot substitute for the records needed to evaluate the actual acquisition.
