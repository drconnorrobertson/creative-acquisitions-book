The First 100 Days After You Buy a Business

The first 100 days are a planning horizon, not a period during which every change should wait. After an acquisition, urgent safety, payroll, customer, security or cash issues may need immediate attention. Other changes should follow a review of the records, people and dependencies. A calm transition starts with knowing which decisions are yours to make and which obligations are already due.

  • Confirm the handoff
  • Communicate verified facts
  • Review the 13-week cash exercise
  • Test knowledge transfer
  • Track unresolved decisions

Confirm control and deadlines at closing

Reconcile the closing package to the actual business: ownership and signatory authority, bank access, payroll provider, insurance, payment approvals, accounting access, essential systems, licenses, leases and required consents. Assign a responsible person and due date to each unresolved item. Do not assume a signed purchase agreement means every operational account or permission transferred.

Use authorized processes to change access and preserve records. Separate preparing a payment, approving it and reconciling it where practical; owner oversight need not mean one person holds every credential or initiates every transaction. Escalate an immediate operating or security problem through the responsible people rather than waiting for an arbitrary day in the transition calendar.

Communicate what has actually been decided

Plan employee and customer communications with the seller and appropriate advisers. Explain the ownership change, current contacts, near-term responsibilities and where questions should go. Do not promise that jobs, pricing, service or reporting lines will never change unless that statement is authorized and supported. State what remains under review and when an update is expected.

Prioritize conversations by dependency and risk rather than a universal employee count or customer percentage. Identify single points of failure, contract-renewal dates, service interruptions, payroll issues and relationships that depend on the seller. Record questions and commitments. A retention bonus or new employment arrangement needs its own approval, terms and funding; a conversation alone does not secure retention.

A weekly forecast uses actual payment dates

Place customer receipts, payroll, tax payments, vendors, rent, capital needs and debt payments in the weeks when cash is expected to move. A weekly forecast does not mean the loan itself is paid weekly. Use executed loan and seller-note schedules, standby restrictions and actual due dates. Compare the seller-note payment calendar and SBA acquisition review.

Original fictional exercise. Two alternative 13-week forecasts start with $100,000 usable opening cash. Base receipts are $30,000 per week; downside receipts are $25,000. Both pay $20,000 weekly payroll and $8,000 other weekly operating cash costs. Assume permitted debt payments of $10,000 in weeks 4, 8 and 12, a $15,000 one-time cost in week 1 and $20,000 capital spending in week 6. These are invented cash inputs, not recommended costs or actual loan terms.

All listed outflows are paid when scheduled; no extra borrowing, owner distributions, taxes or other omitted items are modeled. The opening balance is assumed available after closing. Actual forecasts must include all relevant obligations, restricted cash, receipt timing and minimum cash requirements. The table is not a solvency opinion or a complete operating budget.

The $65,000 difference is the $5,000 weekly receipt reduction multiplied by 13. The negative balance is an unresolved funding gap in the exercise, not permission to delay payroll or another obligation. Identify actions, authorizations and financing availability before cash is needed. Even positive modeled ending cash does not establish that all actual obligations are covered.

Test the handoff while support is available

Turn seller knowledge into specific tasks: complete a renewal, resolve a customer issue, process a vendor order or produce a cash report. Identify the record, access, responsible employee and evidence of completion for each. Follow the actual transition agreement's scope and dates rather than assuming the seller is available for, or disappears after, exactly 100 days.

The seller-transition review separates a handoff promise from a tested task. Schedule sessions for open dependencies and record items the seller cannot resolve. Preserve an escalation route for exceptions; being able to answer every question without help is not a necessary or sufficient test of a sound transition.

Use a review cadence and keep exceptions visible

Compare actual and expected cash movements regularly, explain material differences and roll the forecast forward. Review unresolved customer, employee, contract, access and compliance items with the responsible people. Do not describe an acquisition as successful solely because a day-100 retention or cash target was met.

Choose operating changes based on evidence, urgency, authority, cost and dependencies. Define the expected effect and how it will be checked. A growth initiative should not hide an unresolved payroll, service or cash problem. Continue with the working-capital review and acquisition funding records.
