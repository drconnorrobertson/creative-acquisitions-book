The Search Fund Model: A Step-by-Step Framework for First-Time Acquirers

A search fund separates the work of finding a company from financing and operating an acquisition. In an investor-funded search, initial capital supports a search budget; buying a company requires another decision and financing package. A funded search is not a funded acquisition, and neither removes the possibility of loss.

  • Read research accurately
  • Model the search budget
  • Separate funding decisions
  • Review ownership and obligations
  • Prepare the records

Historical returns are not a median acquisition result

Stanford GSB's July 2026 research summary reports a 33.9% aggregate IRR and 4.75x ROI as of December 31, 2025. It also reports an aggregate acquisition rate of 58%, with about half of the 2021–2024 launch cohorts acquiring a company. These figures describe the study's search-fund population, not a promised return for a new fund or a median acquisition IRR above 30%.

Keep the population, observation date, measure and outcome together when reading a study. Investor returns, the searcher's compensation and the value of vested operator equity are different measures. A historical aggregate cannot supply a personal forecast or prove that every search ends in ownership.

Calculate runway from a stated budget

Original fictional budget exercise. The following alternatives assume a cash-funded search, constant monthly cash spending and a separate one-time reserve spent before calculating runway. They exclude investment returns, taxes, acquisition costs, new fundraising and income from other work. These inputs are invented, not typical fundraising sizes or recommended personal budgets.

None of these rows predicts when a company will be found or whether a purchase will close.

Download the fictional search-budget cases (CSV). The blank reader-review column is for questions and assumptions, not an investment commitment. Actual spending is uneven; retain a dated cash forecast and define the decision needed before available cash is exhausted.

Treat search, acquisition and operation as separate gates

  1. Search funding: establish permitted uses, fees, compensation, reporting, duration and what happens if no acquisition closes. Read investor rights rather than assuming a future obligation to invest.
  2. Target review: test earnings, customer concentration, working capital, capital needs, transition dependencies and valuation. A revenue range or EBITDA margin alone does not establish affordability.
  3. Acquisition financing: obtain commitments for the actual purchase, costs and working cash. Distinguish outside equity, funded debt, seller financing and conditional indications. Compare the funding records.
  4. Operating ownership: reconcile investor preferences, governance, vesting, dilution, exit rights and the operator's employment terms. A headline common-equity percentage does not establish cash proceeds on exit.

There is no required universal funnel from hundreds of targets to one closing. Track your own responses, qualified conversations, diligence costs and reasons for declining opportunities. More outreach does not cure an unsuitable target or missing financing.

Investor funding does not eliminate personal exposure

Review cash invested, forgone earnings, household runway, guarantees and contractual obligations separately. A self-funded search and an investor-funded search can place different obligations on the operator; the labels alone do not establish retained control, limited liability or available capital.

The personal-guarantee review explains why loan obligations need their own analysis. If SBA financing is proposed, use the current SBA acquisition guide and lender review; seller earnouts cannot simply be layered into an SBA change-of-ownership loan. See the current SBA SOP.

Create a decision record before soliciting commitments

Prepare the search budget, funding documents, investor communications, target-selection rationale, diligence records and proposed acquisition sources and uses. Have the appropriate advisers review fundraising, securities, tax and employment issues before relying on a structure or contacting investors with an offer. The guide does not offer an investment or tell a reader which funding route to choose.

After a purchase, maintain a transition plan, cash reporting and authority for operating changes. Continue with management-buyout review and partner-capital records. These are website learning resources, not evidence of a particular book template or a completed acquisition.
