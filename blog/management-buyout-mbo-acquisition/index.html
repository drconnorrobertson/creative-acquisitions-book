Management Buyouts: How Operators Buy the Business They Already Run

An operator may know the business's daily work well and still lack the records needed to buy it. A management buyout (MBO) is an acquisition by existing management, potentially of some or all of the owner's interest. Familiarity helps frame questions; it does not establish earnings quality, transferable contracts, a fair price or available financing.

  • Test insider assumptions
  • Check financing
  • Separate the ownership stages
  • Compare readiness records
  • Prepare the owner conversation

Turn operating knowledge into an independent review

Write down what you know directly, what the owner has represented and what requires independent confirmation. A manager who sees sales reports may not see tax filings, related-party charges, debt covenants, pending claims or the seller's proposed adjustments. Reconcile earnings to source records and cash. Review customer concentration, working capital, capital expenditure and the cost of replacing duties the seller performs.

Continuity does not eliminate retention risk. Ask which relationships depend on the departing owner, which contracts require consent, and who will cover work during negotiation and transition. Agree on authorized access and confidentiality before sharing company records with a lender or potential investor. Counsel should address conflicts between your current role and proposed buyer role.

Confirm the financing route before promising terms

The SBA lender guidance lists Standard 7(a) loans up to $5 million with a 75% maximum SBA guarantee. The 90% guarantee available under certain export programs is not the ordinary Standard 7(a) acquisition guarantee. The guarantee supports the lender; it does not erase the borrower's debt. Loan size, proceeds, repayment, collateral and personal guarantees require their own underwriting.

Use the current SOP 50 10, version 8.1 with technical updates, effective October 1, 2026. Appendix 15 distinguishes Initial Acquisition, Business Expansion, Owner Buyout and ESOP/cooperative categories. An employee's MBO label does not itself establish the applicable category or equity requirement. The Initial Acquisition baseline is 10% of total project costs, not simply 10% of the seller's price; other categories have conditional rules.

Seller debt counted toward required equity must be subordinated and on full standby for the 7(a) term. Limited equity sources together can cover no more than half the required injection. That is different from saying every seller note in every transaction must receive no payments for a decade. The current change-of-ownership rules prohibit seller earnouts. Obtain lender review before combining a note, contingent price or staged ownership with SBA financing.

Build a sources-and-uses schedule including acquisition costs and working cash. Separate cash that will actually arrive at closing from a noncash seller note. Then model operating cash after compensation, capital needs and all permitted debt payments. The SBA acquisition guide and finance-stack review provide the next records to prepare. No financing mix here is an approval or a recommendation to use retirement funds.

Each ownership stage needs its own terms

A minority purchase, an equity grant, a vesting arrangement and a later buyout are different events. For each proposed stage, identify the interest, price or compensation treatment, vesting conditions, voting rights, distributions, transfer restrictions, departure provisions and required consents. Review the legal and tax consequences before issuing or transferring an interest.

Do not assume that time served, EBITDA growth or an initial minority stake guarantees funding for a later purchase. A future price can change. A vested interest may be illiquid and need not supply cash for an equity injection. A future lender can reassess the business, the owners and the applicable rules. Resolve how employment termination, a missed milestone or a declined loan affects any existing interest and future purchase obligation.

Three fictional records can point to different next steps

These are original fictional review prompts, not completed acquisitions or success-rate evidence. All three operators want to buy the same imagined company; each record answers a different question.

Three fictional MBO readiness records
CaseRecord availableStill unresolvedNext review step
A: operating familiarityMonthly sales dashboard and manager's observationsTax-to-ledger reconciliation, owner adjustments and obligationsObtain authorized financial diligence; do not substitute familiarity for verified earnings
B: proposed seller noteA price and unsigned note term sheetLender treatment, payment permissions, funding gap and closing conditionsReconcile cash uses and submit the actual terms for lender and adviser review
C: proposed minority stakeA draft grant with future performance milestonesVesting, rights on departure, future price and ability to fund a later purchaseReview each ownership stage separately; a draft grant is not cash or loan approval

An operator could hold all three records and still have unresolved issues. Track each question, its supporting document, the responsible reviewer and the outstanding decision. A signed purchase agreement, funded closing and effective ownership transfer are distinct from a conversation, draft or indication of interest.

Prepare the negotiation without assuming agreement

  • Set a separate meeting and agree on who represents the seller, buyer and company. Existing employment does not settle those roles.
  • Support valuation with records and stated assumptions. An appraisal can inform negotiation; it cannot guarantee agreement.
  • Document compensation, interim authority, distributions and access to information while the owner retains control.
  • List conditions for financing, diligence, consents and closing. Identify what happens if a condition fails.
  • Review the purchase agreement, employment arrangements, seller obligations and transition plan together with the appropriate advisers.
